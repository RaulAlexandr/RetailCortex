## 1. Florida seaports (volumes 2023-2026, partners, operators, feeder/Miami River, reefer, RoRo, Puerto Rico Jones Act) and Gulf/Southeast competitors

### Takeaway
Florida's three big box ports each handled about 1.1–1.4M TEU in FY2025, about 3.67M TEU combined, all records or near-records. JAXPORT is the largest by TEU, largely because of its dominant Jones Act Puerto Rico lane. PortMiami grows slowly (about 2%/yr) because it is land-constrained and cruise-heavy, and Port Canaveral overtook it as the world's #1 cruise port in FY2025. The smaller Gulf ports (Manatee, Tampa Bay) are building Mexico-focused services, and Palm Beach and the Miami River serve the Caribbean small-island and Haiti trades.

### Cited Findings
**PortMiami**
- FY2025 (Oct 2024–Sep 2025): 1,115,058 TEUs vs 1,089,443 in FY2024 (+2.35%), the 11th consecutive year above 1M TEU — [Miami-Dade County release, Dec 2, 2025](https://www.miamidade.gov/global/release.page%3FMduid_release%3Drel1764622080449470); [WorldPorts](https://www.worldports.org/portmiami-surpasses-one-million-teus-handled-during-fiscal-year-2025). Container Magazine reports the prior year as 1,098,443 (≈2% growth). The two prior-year figures conflict, and the county number is preferred — [Container Magazine](https://www.container-mag.com/article/portmiami-record-cargo-growth-and-cruise-passenger-high)
- FY2025 cruise: record 8,564,225 passengers (+4.02% from 8,233,056). Port Canaveral posted 8,602,047 and became the world's busiest cruise port. PortMiami set a single-day record of 75,201 passengers on Nov 30, 2025, with 10 ships turning around simultaneously — [Miami-Dade release](https://www.miamidade.gov/global/release.page%3FMduid_release%3Drel1764622080449470); [Seatrade Cruise](https://www.seatrade-cruise.com/ports-destinations/canaveral-2025-s-busiest-cruise-port-topping-portmiami)
- In 2023, Florida East Coast Railway (FEC) on-dock rail carried about 5% of PortMiami volume. Infrastructure work was meant to raise capacity to up to 4 trains/day — [Miami Today](https://www.miamitodaynews.com/?p=77687)

**Port Everglades (Broward)**

### Inferences
- Combined FY2025 TEU for the three large Florida ports is ≈3.67M (1.115M + 1.168M + 1.389M), small next to Savannah or Houston (each well above 4M TEU per background knowledge, not re-verified). Florida is a corridor of many mid-sized, fragmented gateways rather than one mega-hub. That favors multi-port visibility and booking tools over single-terminal software.
- JAXPORT's Jones Act PR lane is a captive, domestic, regulated niche: three carriers and a structural northbound-empties imbalance. Backhaul matching, empty repositioning and PR distribution visibility are candidate data problems.
- Manatee and Tampa Bay are building Mexico short-sea services (World Direct, ZIM–Altamira), and FEC/Grupo México has talked about Miami–Veracruz. A Gulf short-sea "Mexico ↔ Florida" alternative to Laredo trucking is emerging but small. It is a possible niche for multimodal routing and quoting.
- The Miami River and Palm Beach (Tropical) trades serve islands with weak port IT, informal documentation and now added Haiti security paperwork. This is a long-tail segment that big platforms (Flexport etc.) usually ignore.

## 2. Air: Miami International Airport (MIA) and alternatives

### Takeaway
MIA is the #1 US airport for international freight: about 2.89M t international and about 3.4M t total in 2025, the sixth straight record year with roughly 13-14% growth. It is the dominant air gateway for LatAm, with Colombia #1 by weight and Brazil #1 by value. The county itself says MIA "would begin to reach capacity at three million tonnes", and 2025 volume already exceeded that. The approved $400M Vertical Integrated Cargo Community (VICC) is targeted for about 2029, so MIA faces about three years of capacity-constrained operation. Perishables (flowers about 90% of the US Valentine's flow), de minimis disruption and tariffs are the main swing factors.

The figures differ by counting method — [Airport Improvement](https://airportimprovement.com/article/mia-soars-to-sixth-straight-record-year-in-cargo-growth/)

### Inferences
- 2025 throughput (3.4M t) already exceeds the about 3M t level the county flagged as the start of capacity constraint, and new capacity won't arrive until about 2029. That points to persistent congestion: truck queues at cargo-city warehouses, ULD build-up and breakdown backlogs, cooler space scarcity at peaks such as Valentine's and Mother's Day, and ramp/parking slot shortages. This supports demand for dock/truck appointment systems, warehouse slotting, peak forecasting and cooler-capacity marketplaces at MIA. These are classic data/ML problems with clear ROI per hour of dwell for perishables.
- Flower inspection relies on sampling (about 20% of boxes) by CBP Agriculture/USDA under extreme seasonal peaks (about 1B stems in 6 weeks). Risk-based pre-arrival data (grower history, pest interception history, farm certification) could cut dwell. A product would need CBP/USDA buy-in, through a pilot with an importer association (AFIF) or an airline such as Avianca.
- Tariffs on Colombia/Ecuador and Colombia's minimum-wage rise squeeze importer margins, which makes spoilage-reduction and landed-cost tools more valuable.
- De minimis ended in Aug 2025 and was made indefinite in June 2026. LatAm cross-border e-commerce sellers who relied on Section 321 now need formal or informal entries, which creates demand for low-cost consolidated-entry and brokerage APIs for LatAm SME sellers shipping via MIA.

## 3. Latin America side: ports, Panama Canal and CK Hutchison, Chancay, security, cold chain

### Takeaway
2026 has been eventful for the LatAm port map. Panama's Supreme Court annulled CK Hutchison's Balboa and Cristóbal concession (Jan 29, 2026), and the state took over the terminals (Feb 23, 2026), with APM Terminals and MSC/TiL reported as temporary operators. Hutchison went to arbitration, and the BlackRock–MSC purchase of Hutchison's global ports is reportedly proceeding *without* Panama. The Panama Canal fully recovered from the 2023-24 drought, with record revenue in FY2026, but an El Niño restriction risk is building. Cosco's Chancay (Peru) passed ~350k TEU in its first year, creating a direct Asia–South America route that bypasses Panama and US hubs. Ecuadorian ports remain a cocaine export hub, with multi-tonne seizures in banana containers through 2025.

**Panama Canal**

Revenue was B/. 6.6B (+16.6%) — [AJOT](https://www.ajot.com/news/panama-canal-reports-growth-in-transits-and-tonnage-in-fiscal-year-2026); [FreshPlaza](https://www.freshplaza.com/asia/article/9885283/panama-canal-transits-rise-4-1-in-fiscal-year-2026/). ## 4. Nearshoring to Mexico: trucking vs rail, Laredo, USMCA review, tariffs, customs brokerage, Carta Porte

### Takeaway
US–Mexico trade hit a record ≈$872B in 2025. Laredo handled ≈$354B (+4.4%), with trucks carrying about three-quarters of bilateral freight value and rail about 11-13%. The tariff war pushed USMCA utilization from ~45% to ~86-89% of Mexican imports, because USMCA-qualifying goods were exempted from the IEEPA fentanyl tariff, the Section 122 surcharge and the new July 2026 Section 301 tariffs. That turned rules-of-origin compliance into the key cost lever. At the July 1, 2026 joint review the US declined to extend USMCA, which triggers annual reviews until 2036. The agreement stays in force, but this guarantees recurring uncertainty. On the Mexican side, Carta Porte digital waybill enforcement keeps shifting (penalty deferral in June 2026), and Mexico raised its own tariffs on non-FTA imports in January 2026.

## 6. US federal regulation affecting the corridor (FMC, CBP, tariffs, USDA/FDA, Jones Act, grants)

### Takeaway
2025-26 was the most turbulent period for US trade rules in decades. IEEPA tariffs were imposed, then struck down by the Supreme Court (Feb 20, 2026, *Learning Resources v. Trump*), which opened about $175B in potential refunds via the CIT and CBP. They were replaced by a temporary 10% Section 122 surcharge (Feb 24–Jul 24, 2026), then by Section 301 "forced labor" tariffs of 10-12.5% on 60 economies (Jul 23, 2026, no expiry, already litigated), plus an extra 25% Section 301 tariff on Brazil (37.5% combined). De minimis was suspended for all countries from Aug 29, 2025 and made indefinite by CBP rule (Jun 24, 2026). FDA delayed FSMA 204 traceability to Jul 20, 2028, although retailers are already demanding lot-level data. FMC's D&D billing rule (2024) is in force but its billing-party provision was vacated by the D.C. Circuit (Oct 2025). EPA Clean Ports grants (~$3B) largely survived, with 53 projects in implementation.

### Cited Findings
**IEEPA tariffs and the Supreme Court**
- Feb 20, 2026: in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* the Supreme Court held 6-3 that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs. It affirmed the CAFC, dismissed the DDC case, and left refunds to the CIT and CAFC. ### Inferences
- In 18 months the tariff legal basis changed four times: IEEPA (2025) → Section 122 (Feb–Jul 2026) → Section 301 forced-labor (Jul 2026–) plus country-specific 301s (Brazil) and Section 232 product tariffs, with de minimis reworked in parallel. Every change requires reclassification, new HTS Chapter 99 codes, landed-cost recalculation and new broker workflows. The roughly $175B of IEEPA refund claims (filed via CIT/CBP and requiring entry-level reconciliation of ACE data) is a time-boxed, very large opportunity for refund-recovery analytics (entry matching, liquidation status, protest deadlines), probably a 2026-27 window.
- For Miami specifically, Brazil at 37.5% is a major hit. Brazil is Miami's #1 trade partner ($19.4B) and MIA's #1 LatAm partner by value ($17.05B). Expect rerouting, product-mix changes and demand for tariff-scenario modelling among Miami forwarders and importers of Brazilian goods.
- The 2028 FSMA 204 deadline plus earlier retailer EDI demands creates a window for lot-level traceability tools for LatAm produce and seafood exporters (many small Spanish-speaking growers) that ship via Florida ports and MIA. Note that cut flowers are not food and so are not covered by FSMA 204.
- The D&D billing rule's partial vacatur leaves disputes complaint-driven and document-heavy. Shippers need automated invoice auditing against OSRA-required fields and free-time and terminal-availability evidence. Products such as BlueCargo's OSRA tools exist ([BlueCargo OSRA FAQ](https://bluecargo.io/osra-faq)), but there is room for small-shipper and forwarder-focused versions.
- Dense commercial tariff-tracking content (TariffLens, GingerControl, CalcMyTariff, Tradlinx blogs) shows that tariff-intelligence SaaS is already a crowded, low-barrier category. Differentiation would need deep integration (ACE data, broker workflows) or a corridor-specific angle such as USMCA origin or LatAm SME exporters.

## 7. Biggest operational pain points where software/data could help

### Takeaway
The pain points most backed by evidence from this research are: (1) tariff and regulatory volatility (four legal bases in 18 months, ~$175B refunds, Brazil at 37.5%, USMCA annual reviews); (2) MIA physical capacity saturation (3.4M t vs a ~3M t threshold, new capacity ~2029) under extreme perishables peaks with sampled CBP/USDA inspection; (3) documentation burden on small LatAm exporters and sellers (USMCA certificates, de minimis loss, FSMA 204 lot data, Carta Porte, Haiti vessel security docs); (4) security and integrity risks (cocaine in Ecuadorian reefer containers); and (5) network volatility (Panama Canal El Niño risk, Panama terminal operator change, Chancay rerouting). Cargo theft and Miami forwarder fragmentation are plausible but were not quantified in this session.

### Inferences — ranked opportunity hypotheses for a data/ML/software founder
1. **Corridor-specific trade-compliance copilot (Spanish/Portuguese/English).** Covers HS classification, USMCA origin qualification from BOMs, tariff-stack calculation (232/301/AD-CVD; Brazil 37.5%), and supplier declarations collected in Spanish via WhatsApp/web. *Why now:* annual USMCA reviews, the 301 regime and de minimis loss. *Buyers:* Miami/Doral and Laredo customs brokers and forwarders, Mexican maquilas, LatAm SME exporters. *Risk:* a crowded tariff-content category, so it needs workflow depth (ACE/ABI integration via a broker partner, VUCEM/pedimento on the MX side).
2. **IEEPA refund-recovery and entry-reconciliation analytics.** Matches ACE entry data to IEEPA Chapter 99 lines, tracks liquidation and protest deadlines, and files via brokers. A large but time-boxed opportunity (2026-27), and a possible beachhead to win broker relationships.
3. **MIA perishables flow optimization.** Truck and dock appointment scheduling across cargo-city warehouses, cooler-capacity forecasting and marketplace, ULD/warehouse dwell prediction, and peak (Valentine's, Mother's Day) planning. Shares data with airlines (Avianca, LATAM, Atlas), handlers and importers (AFIF). MIA will be capacity-constrained until ~2029. *Risk:* incumbents such as Descartes, CargoWise and handler-specific systems, and the need for airport-authority cooperation.
4. **Risk-based inspection pre-clearance data for agricultural imports.** Grower and farm history, pest-interception history and certifications feed a risk score that helps CBP/USDA prioritize sampling. Requires a government partnership, so it is a slower and harder sale, but it is defensible.
5. **Lot-level traceability (FSMA 204) and integrity platform for LatAm produce and seafood exporters**, with cocaine-contamination chain-of-custody evidence (seals, photos, IoT) bundled in. Spanish-first, mobile and WhatsApp-native for small growers. The commercial deadline is earlier than 2028 because retailers ask for EDI lot data now.
6. **Cross-border Mexico document orchestration.** Carta Porte validation, pedimento ↔ US entry ↔ BOL consistency checks, and transfer-carrier (transfer/drayage) handoff visibility at Laredo, where about 20k trucks cross per day. Competes with Nuvocargo-type managed marketplaces, so it would be a SaaS layer rather than a broker.
7. **Network risk forecasting.** Panama Canal draft and slot prediction (El Niño), terminal-change alerts and Chancay/alternative routing scenarios, sold to BCOs and forwarders shipping between the US East Coast/Gulf, Asia and west-coast South America.
8. **Caribbean small-island and Haiti long-tail.** Booking, documentation (including the new USCG Haiti requirements) and consolidation tools for Miami River and Palm Beach small-vessel operators and their small shippers. Underserved but small and risky (Haiti security).

### Inferences
- Funding for LatAm logistics tech appears to have peaked in 2021-22 (Nowports $1.1B; Nuvocargo backed by Tiger Global), with few large rounds visible since. This matches the broader post-2022 freight-tech downturn (Convoy's collapse, Flexport's restructuring, per background knowledge). A new entrant should expect capital-efficient, SaaS-first and revenue-early expectations from investors (LAVCA-type funds, QED, ALLVP, Kaszek) rather than asset-heavy digital-forwarder models.
- Nowports and Nuvocargo are *service-heavy* (digital forwarder, managed trucking marketplace). The gap is *software and data infrastructure* sold to the many existing small forwarders, brokers and exporters: compliance automation, document AI for Spanish/Portuguese commercial documents (facturas, pedimentos, BL, certificados fitosanitarios), and perishables flow optimization at MIA.
- No dedicated player was found for: (a) MIA cargo-community digitalization (truck appointments, cooler capacity); (b) the Caribbean small-island and Miami River trade; (c) LatAm grower-side FSMA 204 traceability; (d) the IEEPA refund-recovery workflow for small and mid-size importers. All are candidate wedges, though absence in a limited search is not proof of absence.
